Welford’s Recurrence
Formulas

Computing mean and variance incrementally, then using repeated samples to understand how statistical estimates behave as the sample size increases.

Online algorithms Sampling Inferential statistics
01
BACKGROUND

Introduction

When analysing a large number of observations, it is often useful to update statistical quantities as new data arrive. A straightforward approach would be to store every observation and recompute the mean and variance whenever a new value is added. However, this becomes inefficient when datasets are large or when observations arrive continuously.

Welford’s recurrence formulas solve this problem by updating the mean and the sum of squared deviations using only the previous state and the newly observed value. The method requires constant memory, performs a small number of operations per observation, and is numerically more stable than some direct formulas for variance.

In the second exercise, the method is applied to a simulation in which several hackers independently attempt to penetrate systems. Each attempt produces a score between 0 and 10. By comparing the statistics computed from each hacker’s trajectory with the known theoretical values, we can study how reliable sample estimates become as the number of observations increases.

02
EXERCISE 1

Welford’s recurrence formulas

Consider a sequence of observations x₁, x₂, …, xₙ. After processing the first n−1 values, suppose that we already know their mean μₙ₋₁ and their sum of squared deviations SSₙ₋₁. We now want to include a new observation xₙ, without going back through the previous data.

Updating the mean

The mean of the first n observations is the previous mean corrected by the difference between the new value and that previous mean. This difference is often called the delta. Dividing it by the new sample size ensures that the correction becomes smaller as more observations are collected.

MEAN RECURRENCE
μₙ = μₙ₋₁ + (xₙ − μₙ₋₁) / n

Updating the sum of squares

Define SSₙ as the sum of squared distances of the observations from their updated mean. When the new observation is included, all deviations are now measured relative to μₙ rather than μₙ₋₁. Welford’s key result expresses this change using only the old mean, the new mean and the new observation:

SUM OF SQUARED DEVIATIONS
SSₙ = SSₙ₋₁ + (xₙ − μₙ₋₁)(xₙ − μₙ)

The first factor measures how far the new observation is from the old mean; the second measures how far it is from the updated mean. Their product is exactly the correction that must be added to the previous sum of squares. We initialise the algorithm with n = 0, μ₀ = 0 and SS₀ = 0.

Computing the variance

Once all observations have been processed, the sample variance is obtained by dividing the accumulated sum of squares by n−1. This denominator, known as Bessel’s correction, makes the sample variance an unbiased estimator of the population variance when observations are independent and identically distributed with finite variance.

SAMPLE VARIANCE
s²ₙ = SSₙ / (n − 1), for n ≥ 2

The core update consists of three operations: increment the count, update the mean using the old delta, and update the sum of squares using the new mean. No previous observation needs to be stored.

WELFORD UPDATE CORE ALGORITHM 
n += 1
delta = x - mean
mean += delta / n
M2 += delta * (x - mean)

# At the end of the stream:
sample_variance = M2 / (n - 1)  # if n >= 2

Here, M2 stores the running sum of squared deviations, equivalent to SSₙ.

EXERCISE 2

Sampling mean and variance

We now extend the previous setting. There are m hackers, and each hacker attempts to penetrate n systems. Every attempt receives a score between 0 and 10. For this experiment, scores are independent draws from a discrete uniform distribution: each integer from 0 through 10 is equally likely.

This is a convenient choice because its theoretical properties are known in advance. It allows us to check whether the statistics estimated from the simulated trajectories agree with the population parameters.

CHOSEN DISTRIBUTION

Discrete uniform

Every score from 0 to 10 has probability 1/11.

01234 5678910
THEORETICAL MEAN
μ = 5

The average of the eleven possible scores.

THEORETICAL VARIANCE
σ² = 10

The expected squared distance from the population mean.

From individual trajectories to overall estimates

Each hacker generates a trajectory containing n scores, one for each attempted system. Welford’s algorithm is applied independently to each trajectory, producing one sample mean and one sample variance per hacker. We then average these m sample means and, separately, these m sample variances.

The average of the sample means should be close to the theoretical mean μ = 5. Likewise, because we use the sample variance with denominator n−1, the average of the sample variances should be close to the theoretical variance σ² = 10. With a finite number of hackers, the averages will not generally be exactly equal to their theoretical values; random variation is expected.

AVERAGES ACROSS HACKERS
μ̄ = (1/m) Σ μⱼ     s̄² = (1/m) Σ sⱼ²

Why does increasing n matter?

The number of systems attempted by each hacker determines the sample size of each trajectory. As n grows, each hacker’s sample mean becomes less sensitive to the particular scores observed. In fact, its variance is σ²/n = 10/n, so the standard deviation of the sample means decreases proportionally to 1/√n.

The sample variance is also an unbiased estimate of σ², and its variability decreases as the sample size increases. Consequently, the sample variances tend to cluster more tightly around 10. Increasing m does something different: it gives us more independent estimates to average, making the overall averages more stable, while leaving the variability of an individual hacker’s estimate unchanged.

05
INTERPRETATION

Discussion and conclusions

The experiment illustrates two important properties of sample statistics. First, the sample mean and the sample variance are unbiased estimators of their corresponding population parameters. Across many independent hackers, their average estimates should therefore approach 5 and 10, respectively. Small differences are normal and are caused by the randomness of the simulated samples.

Second, increasing the number of systems attempted by each hacker makes the estimates more precise. The distributions of sample means and sample variances become narrower, meaning that individual estimates are less likely to be far from the theoretical values. Increasing the number of hackers improves the precision of the averages across hackers, whereas increasing the number of systems improves the precision of each hacker’s estimate.

These properties are central to inferential statistics. In real applications, the distribution of an entire population is usually unknown, so we use a finite sample to estimate quantities such as its mean and variance. Welford’s algorithm provides an efficient way to compute these quantities as data arrive, while the simulation demonstrates why larger samples generally lead to more reliable estimates.

KEY TAKEAWAY

More observations per hacker reduce the variability of each hacker’s estimates. More hackers make the averages across hackers more stable. Together, these effects show how sample size and repeated sampling support statistical inference.
