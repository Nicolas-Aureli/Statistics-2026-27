THEORETICAL BACKGROUND

Modes of convergence

A sequence of random variables can approach a limit in different mathematical senses. These distinctions determine which conclusions can be drawn from a sequence of statistical estimates.

Convergence in probability

A sequence of random variables Xₙ converges in probability to a random variable X when the probability that their difference exceeds any fixed positive threshold becomes arbitrarily small as n increases.

Definition Xₙ →ᵖ X if P(|Xₙ − X| > ε) → 0 for every ε > 0

This means that large deviations from the limiting value become increasingly unlikely. It does not require every individual sequence of outcomes to converge.

Convergence in probability is the foundation of many consistency results in statistics, including the Weak Law of Large Numbers.

Almost sure convergence

Almost sure convergence is a stronger form of convergence. Xₙ converges almost surely to X if the sequence converges to X for all outcomes except possibly those belonging to an event with probability zero.

Definition P(limₙ→∞ Xₙ = X) = 1

Unlike convergence in probability, almost sure convergence describes the long-term behaviour of individual sample paths. With probability one, the sequence eventually approaches its limit and remains arbitrarily close.

Almost sure convergence is closely associated with the Strong Law of Large Numbers.

Mean-square convergence (L²)

A sequence converges in mean square when the expected squared difference between Xₙ and X approaches zero. This criterion considers both the magnitude of errors and their probability.

Definition E[(Xₙ − X)²] → 0

Mean-square convergence is useful because it allows us to study errors through their second moments. It is particularly important when analyzing estimators, variances and stochastic processes.

If Xₙ converges in L² to X, then it also converges in probability to X. This follows from Markov's inequality applied to the squared difference.

Implication E[(Xₙ − X)²] → 0   ⇒   Xₙ →ᵖ X

Convergence in distribution

Convergence in distribution occurs when the cumulative distribution functions of a sequence converge to the cumulative distribution function of a limiting random variable at every continuity point of that function.

Definition Xₙ →ᵈ X if Fₙ(x) → F(x) at every continuity point of F

This form of convergence concerns the overall distribution of the random variables rather than the closeness of individual outcomes.

The Central Limit Theorem is one of the most important examples of convergence in distribution.

How are the four modes related?

Some forms of convergence imply others, but the implications cannot generally be reversed.

↓ implies
Probability Convergence in probability
↓ implies

Almost sure convergence and L² convergence are not generally comparable without additional assumptions. In particular, almost sure convergence does not necessarily imply mean-square convergence.

ASYMPTOTIC RESULTS

The Law of Large Numbers

Repeated observations help stabilize the sample mean around the expected value of the underlying process.

Weak Law of Large Numbers

Let X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ be independent and identically distributed random variables with finite expectation μ and finite variance σ². Their sample mean converges in probability to μ as the number of observations grows.

Weak Law X̄ₙ →ᵖ μ

This means that the sample average becomes increasingly likely to be close to the population mean.

Strong Law of Large Numbers

Under suitable assumptions, the sample mean converges almost surely to the expected value. In the classical IID setting with a finite expected value, the strong law states:

Strong Law X̄ₙ → μ almost surely

The strong law gives a stronger conclusion about long-term sample paths. With probability one, the empirical average eventually approaches the true mean.

INTERACTIVE EXPERIMENT

Why does √n appear?

The Central Limit Theorem explains why sample means become approximately normally distributed and why their fluctuations shrink at a rate proportional to 1/√n.

Variance of a sample mean

Consider n independent observations with common mean μ and variance σ². The variance of their sum is the sum of their variances:

Variance of the sum Var(X₁ + ··· + Xₙ) = nσ²

The sample mean divides the sum by n. Since variance scales with the square of a constant, dividing by n reduces the variance by n².

Variance of the sample mean Var(X̄ₙ) = nσ² / n² = σ² / n

Taking the square root gives the standard error:

Standard error SE(X̄ₙ) = σ / √n

Therefore, the typical fluctuation of the sample mean decreases proportionally to 1/√n. To reduce the standard error by half, the sample size must be multiplied by four.

Independence is essential for the simple variance additivity above. When observations are correlated, covariance terms must also be considered.

The Central Limit Theorem

The Central Limit Theorem states that, under suitable conditions, the standardized sum of independent random variables converges in distribution to a standard normal random variable.

Central Limit Theorem (X̄ₙ − μ) / (σ / √n) →ᵈ N(0,1)

This result explains why normal approximations appear so frequently in statistics, even when the original observations do not follow a normal distribution.

The sample mean is approximately normally distributed for sufficiently large n, with mean μ and standard deviation σ/√n.

PROBABILITY & INFERENCE

Conditional probability and Bayes

New evidence changes the probability assigned to an event. Bayesian inference formalizes this process by combining prior information with the likelihood of observed data.

Conditional probability

Conditional probability describes the probability of an event A given that another event B has occurred. The information that B occurred changes the sample space considered when evaluating A.

Conditional probability P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

If A and B are independent, knowing that B occurred does not change the probability of A:

Independence P(A | B) = P(A)

Conditional probability is central to statistical inference because observations provide information about unknown quantities and hypotheses.

Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem connects conditional probabilities and allows us to reverse the direction of conditioning. It calculates the probability of a hypothesis given observed evidence.

Bayes' theorem P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B)

When the possible hypotheses form a partition of the sample space, the denominator can be expanded using the law of total probability.

Law of total probability P(B) = Σᵢ P(B | Aᵢ)P(Aᵢ)
Bayesian updating P(Aᵢ | B) = P(B | Aᵢ)P(Aᵢ) / Σⱼ P(B | Aⱼ)P(Aⱼ)

Prior, likelihood and posterior

01

Prior probability

The prior represents the probability assigned to a hypothesis before considering the current evidence. It may be based on historical data, previous experiments or domain knowledge.

02

Likelihood

The likelihood describes how probable the observed evidence is under a particular hypothesis. It measures how well the hypothesis explains the data.

03

Posterior probability

The posterior is the updated probability of a hypothesis after incorporating the evidence. It becomes the new basis for subsequent inference.

A medical testing example

Suppose a disease affects a small percentage of a population. A diagnostic test is highly sensitive, meaning it correctly identifies most individuals who have the disease. However, the test can also produce false positives.

A positive result does not necessarily mean that the individual has the disease. The probability of disease after a positive result depends not only on the accuracy of the test but also on how common the disease is before testing.

Probability of disease given a positive result P(D | +) = P(+ | D)P(D) / P(+)

This is a classic example of why conditional probabilities cannot be reversed without considering the prior probability.

APPLICATIONS

Bayesian inference in practice

Bayesian updating is useful whenever new observations can change the assessment of an uncertain hypothesis.

+

Medical diagnosis

A physician combines the prevalence of a condition, symptoms and test results to update the probability of a diagnosis. The same test may lead to different posterior probabilities in populations with different prior risks.

⌁

Cybersecurity

An intrusion detection system can update the probability that an event represents a genuine attack based on indicators such as unusual traffic, failed logins, known signatures and historical threat information.

↗

Machine learning

Bayesian methods can incorporate prior knowledge into model parameters and update uncertainty when new training data becomes available.

◷

Forecasting

Predictions can be revised as new observations arrive. Bayesian models provide a framework for combining previous estimates with current information.

CRITICAL DISCUSSION

Advantages and limitations

Bayesian inference provides a coherent framework for uncertainty, but its conclusions depend on assumptions and the information used.

+

Advantages

Bayesian methods allow prior information to be incorporated explicitly, provide an intuitive interpretation of posterior probabilities, and offer a natural framework for updating conclusions as new evidence becomes available.

−

Limitations

Results can depend strongly on the chosen prior, particularly when little data is available. Computational methods may also be demanding, and a poorly specified model can produce misleading posterior conclusions.

Final considerations

The different modes of convergence describe distinct aspects of the long-term behaviour of random variables. Convergence in probability and almost sure convergence are particularly important for understanding consistency, while convergence in distribution provides the foundation for the Central Limit Theorem and many approximation techniques used in statistical inference.

The factor √n arises naturally from the variance of sums of independent observations. As the sample size increases, the uncertainty of the sample mean decreases at a rate of 1/√n, explaining why this scaling appears in confidence intervals, hypothesis testing and asymptotic statistical theory.

Bayesian inference approaches uncertainty from a different perspective. Rather than relying exclusively on asymptotic behaviour, it updates probabilities by combining prior knowledge with observed evidence. Its flexibility makes it useful in many applications, including cybersecurity, where decisions often need to be revised as new information becomes available.
